AP Psychology, Unit 6 (Learning): Classical conditioning study guide

Classical conditioning is a type of learning in which an organism learns to associate two stimuli, so that a
stimulus that once meant nothing comes to trigger a response on its own.

Ivan Pavlov, a Russian physiologist studying digestion, noticed that his dogs began to salivate before food
reached their mouths: at the sight of the person who fed them, or the sound of their footsteps.

Key terms
- Unconditioned stimulus (US): a stimulus that triggers a response naturally, without learning. Example: food in
  the mouth.
- Unconditioned response (UR): the unlearned, automatic response to the US. Example: salivating when food is in
  the mouth.
- Neutral stimulus (NS): a stimulus that triggers no response of interest before conditioning. Example: a tone
  before training.
- Conditioned stimulus (CS): the once-neutral stimulus that, after being paired with the US, triggers a response
  on its own.
- Conditioned response (CR): the learned response to the CS. Example: salivating at the tone.

The UR and the CR are often the same behavior (salivating). What differs is what triggers it: the UR is triggered
by the US, the CR by the CS.

Processes
- Acquisition: the stage when the NS is paired with the US again and again, and the association forms. It works
  best when the NS comes just before the US (about half a second), so that it predicts the US.
- Extinction: when the CS is presented again and again without the US, the CR weakens.
- Spontaneous recovery: after a rest, an extinguished CR reappears, usually weaker, when the CS is presented again.
  Extinction suppresses the association rather than erasing it.
- Generalization: the tendency to respond to stimuli similar to the CS (a slightly higher tone).
- Discrimination: the learned ability to tell the CS apart from similar stimuli that do not signal the US.

Cognition matters. Robert Rescorla showed that conditioning depends on how reliably the CS predicts the US, not
just on how often they are paired: the organism learns that the CS signals the US is coming.

Biology matters. John Garcia showed that rats who became sick hours after tasting a flavor avoided that flavor
afterward, sometimes after a single pairing: some associations are learned far more easily than others.

Examples
- Watson and Rayner's "Little Albert" study (1920): a loud noise (US) caused fear (UR). After a white rat (NS) was
  paired with the noise several times, the rat alone (CS) caused fear (CR). Albert's fear generalized to other
  furry objects.
- Feeling hungry when the lunch bell rings; feeling anxious at the sound of a dentist's drill.

Classical vs. operant conditioning: classical conditioning links two stimuli and involves automatic responses;
operant conditioning links a behavior with its consequence (reinforcement or punishment).
